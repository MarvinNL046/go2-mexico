Mexico City, the bustling capital of Mexico, is experiencing rapid subsidence, sinking nearly **10 inches** every year—a process so pronounced that scientists can observe it from space using NASA satellite imagery. This alarming rate of land sinking, driven primarily by excessive groundwater extraction in a city built atop a former lake bed, poses significant challenges to infrastructure and urban life. According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/mexico-city-sinking-nasa-satellite-imagery-b5a70fbc2f73a789f248ab69b08c75e7), the phenomenon underscores long-standing environmental pressures in one of the world's largest metropolitan areas.

## Background on Mexico City's Subsidence

Mexico City's subsidence is not a new issue but has been accelerating due to the city's unique geological setting. Founded by the Aztecs in the 14th century on an island in Lake Texcoco, the area was later drained by Spanish colonizers to create habitable land. Today, much of the city rests on soft, compressible sediments that compact when water is removed from underground aquifers. As the population has grown to over 20 million in the greater metropolitan area, demand for water has led to over-extraction, causing the ground to sink unevenly.

Widely known public data indicates that parts of the city have been subsiding for decades, with some areas dropping as much as 40 feet since the early 20th century. The recent visibility from space comes from advanced satellite technologies, such as NASA's interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR), which measures tiny changes in Earth's surface with millimeter precision. These tools reveal that subsidence rates vary across the city, with the most affected zones in the historic center and eastern boroughs experiencing the highest drops. While the reference to nearly **10 inches** per year represents an average in vulnerable spots, the uneven nature means some buildings tilt or crack as the ground shifts differentially.

This issue is compounded by the city's seismic activity; Mexico City lies in an earthquake-prone region, and sinking land exacerbates risks to structures during tremors. Historical events, like the 1985 earthquake that killed thousands, highlighted how subsidence can amplify damage by altering soil stability. Although exact current measurements are derived from satellite data, the core cause remains groundwater depletion, as surface water sources prove insufficient for the urban sprawl.

## Implications for Infrastructure and Daily Life

The sinking of Mexico City has tangible effects on its infrastructure. Metro lines, roads, and pipelines strain under the shifting earth, leading to frequent repairs and disruptions. For instance, the city's extensive subway system, one of the oldest in Latin America, has seen tracks warp and stations flood due to subsidence-induced settling. Water supply systems are particularly vulnerable; as the ground drops, pipes break more easily, worsening the very water scarcity that fuels the problem.

Residents face challenges too, with uneven subsidence causing buildings to lean or develop fissures. In extreme cases, this has led to the abandonment of structures in heavily affected areas. The city's tilt—once level, now sloping noticeably toward the south—alters drainage patterns, increasing flood risks during rainy seasons. Climate change adds another layer, as rising temperatures may intensify water demands and evaporation rates.

From an environmental perspective, the subsidence contributes to broader ecological strain. Lake Texcoco's remnants, now mostly dry, highlight the long-term consequences of altering natural water systems. Efforts to recharge aquifers with treated wastewater have been piloted, but scaling them citywide remains a challenge amid rapid urbanization.

However, travelers should be aware of potential disruptions: metro lines may experience delays due to maintenance, and some older buildings in the historic center could show visible cracks, though these are monitored by authorities.

When planning trips, consider staying in less affected areas like Polanco or Roma, where subsidence rates are lower compared to the eastern outskirts. ## What's Next for Mexico City

Addressing subsidence requires multifaceted solutions, starting with reducing groundwater reliance. Authorities have invested in desalination plants and rainwater harvesting to supplement supplies, though implementation is slow. Satellite monitoring, as highlighted in the AP News report, will play a crucial role in tracking progress and identifying high-risk zones for targeted interventions.

Urban planning experts advocate for stricter building codes in sinking areas and incentives for relocating water-intensive industries. International collaboration, including with NASA, could enhance data sharing for better predictions. While speculation abounds on timelines—some predict worsening without action—known efforts focus on sustainability. For Mexico City, balancing growth with environmental stewardship will be key to preserving its status as a global hub.

In the coming years, visitors can expect continued improvements in resilience, ensuring the city's allure endures despite its sinking foundations. Travelers are encouraged to support eco-friendly initiatives, like using public transport, to contribute positively during their stay.
