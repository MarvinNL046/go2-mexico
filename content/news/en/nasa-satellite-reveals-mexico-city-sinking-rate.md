Mexico City, home to over 22 million people, is sinking at an alarming rate of nearly **10 inches (25 centimeters)** per year, according to satellite imagery released this week by NASA. The data, captured between October 2025 and January 2026, reveals this one of the world's fastest-subsiding urban areas, driven by extensive groundwater pumping and urban development on the city's ancient lake bed foundation. The findings underscore long-standing geological issues that have plagued the capital for over a century, now documented with unprecedented precision from space.

### Background on Mexico City's Subsidence

Mexico City sprawls across approximately **3,000 square miles (7,800 square kilometers)**, encompassing the capital and surrounding urban areas built atop what was once an ancient lake bed. Historical records show that many downtown streets were originally canals, a legacy that persists in rural outskirts where traditional water channels remain. This soft, compressible soil has long been vulnerable to subsidence, or land sinking, exacerbated by the city's rapid growth and heavy reliance on underground aquifers for water.

For more than a century, excessive groundwater extraction to meet the needs of its massive population has caused the aquifer to contract dramatically. As the water table drops, the surrounding clay and sediment compact, leading to uneven sinking across the region. Iconic landmarks bear the scars of this process: the **Metropolitan Cathedral**, construction of which began in 1573, now tilts visibly to one side. Similarly, the **Angel of Independence** monument has required the addition of **14 steps** to its base over the years to compensate for the dropping ground level around it.

Enrique Cabral, a geophysics researcher at the National Autonomous University of Mexico, describes the issue as a "very big problem." He notes that subsidence has caused the city to drop more than **39 feet (12 meters)** in less than a century in some areas. Overall, the phenomenon ranks Mexico City among the global leaders in subsidence velocity, with rates in certain spots averaging **0.78 inches (2 centimeters)** per month—translating to about **9.5 inches (24 centimeters)** annually.

This isn't a new discovery; subsidence has been monitored for decades. However, the latest data provides a clearer picture of its scale and variability, highlighting hotspots like the main airport and central monuments where sinking exceeds **half an inch (more than 2 centimeters)** monthly.

### NASA's NISAR Satellite: A Game-Changer in Monitoring

The revelations come from NASA's newly released report based on observations from the NISAR satellite, a joint project with the Indian Space Research Organization. Launched to track real-time surface changes on Earth, NISAR uses advanced radar to detect millimeter-level shifts, even in densely urbanized areas like Mexico City. Scientist Paul Rosen explains that the technology goes beyond surface observations, offering insights into subsurface dynamics. "It's basically documentation of all of these changes within a city," Rosen said. "You can see the full magnitude of the problem."

The dark blue zones on NASA's subsidence maps indicate the most affected areas, where monthly drops surpass the half-inch threshold. By providing high-resolution data over short periods, NISAR allows researchers to pinpoint variations that ground-based measurements might miss. In the future, the team aims to refine this to a building-by-building level, enabling more targeted interventions.

Beyond Mexico City, NISAR's capabilities hold promise for global applications. Researchers plan to use it to monitor natural disasters, fault line shifts, and climate change impacts, such as ice melt in Antarctica. It could enhance early warning systems—for instance, alerting governments to potential evacuations during volcanic activity or earthquakes.

### Impacts on Infrastructure and the Water Crisis

The sinking poses severe risks to Mexico City's critical infrastructure. Cabral highlights damage to the subway system, drainage networks, potable water supply, housing, and streets. As the ground shifts unevenly, buildings and pipelines crack, leading to frequent repairs and heightened vulnerability to flooding and seismic events. The contracting aquifer not only drives subsidence but also fuels a chronic water crisis, with shortages expected to intensify as demand grows.

In a city already grappling with overpopulation and pollution, these geological pressures compound everyday challenges. Tilted structures and unstable foundations affect daily life, from commuting via the subway to maintaining historic sites that draw millions of tourists annually. The subsidence exacerbates inequality, as wealthier areas with better engineering fare better than poorer neighborhoods on softer soils.

### What This Means for Visitors and What's Next

For tourists, Mexico City's subsidence serves as a reminder of the city's resilient yet fragile character. Iconic sites like the Angel of Independence remain accessible and awe-inspiring, but awareness of ground instability can inform choices—opting for modern accommodations over historic ones prone to tilting, for example. The water crisis may lead to occasional shortages in hotels or restaurants, so planning around peak seasons helps. Overall, the phenomenon doesn't deter visits but encourages appreciation for sustainable urban planning.

Looking ahead, the NASA data marks a significant step in mitigation efforts. According to Cabral, it advances studies on subsidence, potentially guiding policies to reduce groundwater pumping and reinforce infrastructure. Broader adoption of NISAR technology could help Mexico City—and similar subsiding cities worldwide—adapt to these challenges. As reported by [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/nasa-satellite-reveals-just-how-fast-mexico-city-is-sinking), ongoing monitoring will be crucial to track progress and prevent escalation.

In the long term, addressing the root causes—through alternative water sources and stricter development regulations—will be essential. For now, the satellite's revelations spotlight the urgency, ensuring that one of the world's great metropolises can endure for generations to come.
