Mexico City, one of the world's largest urban centers, is sinking into the ground at rates of up to **2cm** per month in some areas, a phenomenon now being monitored in unprecedented detail by NASA's NISAR satellite mission. Launched as a joint effort with the Indian Space Research Organisation, NISAR provides real-time radar imaging that penetrates clouds and vegetation to track surface changes. This ongoing subsidence, which has persisted for over a century, poses risks to infrastructure and daily life, with visible effects on iconic landmarks, as reported in a recent article by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/may/07/mexico-city-sinking-subsidence-2cm-a-month-nasa-nisar).

### Background on Mexico City's Subsidence

The sinking of [Mexico City](https://go2-mexico.com/city/mexico-city/) is a long-standing issue rooted in the city's unique geology. Built on the dried-up bed of Lake Texcoco in the Valley of Mexico, the metropolis relies heavily on groundwater extraction to meet the needs of its more than 20 million residents. This over-extraction causes the underlying clay-rich soil to compact and settle, leading to subsidence rates that vary across the city but can reach alarming levels.

One of the most striking manifestations is in the historic Zócalo, the capital's vast central plaza. Here, the Mexico City Metropolitan Cathedral, with its towering spires, slumps noticeably to one side. An adjacent structure, the Metropolitan Sanctuary, tilts in the opposite direction, while the nearby National Palace appears off-kilter. These distortions are not mere optical illusions but tangible signs of the ground shifting beneath the 16th-century buildings, which were constructed when the area was still marshy.

This subsidence has been documented for over a century, but ground-based measurements have limitations, especially in sprawling urban environments. Satellite observations began earlier, but they lacked the precision and coverage of newer technologies. The problem extends beyond aesthetics; it threatens pipelines, roads, and buildings, exacerbating issues like flooding in a city already prone to heavy rains and seismic activity. For instance, the city's main airport is among the areas sinking more than **2cm** a month, one of the fastest rates globally, which could impact runway stability and operations over time.

### NASA's NISAR Mission: A Game-Changer in Monitoring

Enter NISAR, a powerful radar system launched into space to detect even the smallest changes in Earth's surface. "Nisar takes radar imaging observations of Earth to the next level," said Marin Govorčin, a scientist at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory. "Nisar will see any change big or small that happens on Earth from week to week. No other imaging mission can claim this."

Unlike previous satellites, NISAR provides data on subsidence across broader areas, including the city's outskirts with complex terrain that was hard to study before. Between October 2025 and January 2026, its imagery revealed dark blue zones on maps indicating subsidence exceeding **2cm** monthly, highlighting hotspots around the airport and central districts. This real-time capability allows for weekly updates, offering a dynamic view of how the sinking evolves.

The mission's dual-frequency radar—L-band from NASA and S-band from ISRO—ensures it can operate regardless of weather, making it ideal for consistent monitoring. A clear example of the subsidence's progress is the Angel of Independence, a 36-meter monument on Paseo de la Reforma completed in 1910. To compensate for the surrounding land sinking, engineers have added **14 steps** to its base over the years, illustrating the gradual but relentless descent.

Darío Solano-Rojas, an engineer at the National Autonomous University of Mexico (Unam), emphasized the mission's potential: "This study of Mexico City speaks to the realm of possibilities that will open up thanks to the NISAR system. And not just for sinking cities but also for studying volcanoes, for studying the deformation associated with earthquakes, for studying landslides."

### Broader Implications and Global Applications

While focused on Mexico City, NISAR's data has far-reaching value. David Bekaert, a project manager at the Flemish Institute for Technological Research and a NISAR science team member, noted, "Images like this are just the beginning. We’re going to see an influx of new discoveries from all over the world." The technology can monitor the climate crisis, glacier movement, agricultural productivity, soil moisture, forestry, coastal flooding, and more.

In Mexico City, the subsidence worsens vulnerabilities to other hazards. The city's soft soil already amplifies earthquakes, and sinking infrastructure could lead to more frequent cracks and failures. Experts hope this detailed tracking will spur action, such as better water management or reinforced building codes, though no specific policy changes are mentioned in current reports.

For the region, this underscores environmental challenges in urban areas built on unstable foundations. Similar issues affect other cities like Jakarta and Venice, and NISAR's global reach could inform international strategies.
