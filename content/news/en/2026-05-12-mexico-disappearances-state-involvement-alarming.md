A new report from the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (IACHR), exclusively accessed by **The Guardian**, warns that state actors in Mexico are involved in enforced disappearances at an "alarming" rate, exacerbating a crisis that has seen more than **130,000** people vanish, primarily over the last 20 years since the government launched its war on drug cartels. Released on May 11, 2026, the investigation details widespread collusion between criminal groups and officials, occurring amid ongoing marches by relatives of the missing in [Mexico City](https://go2-mexico.com/city/mexico-city/), where at least **27** searchers have been killed since 2010.

### Background on Mexico's Disappearance Crisis

Mexico's crisis of disappearances has deep roots, tracing back to the country's "dirty war" in the 1960s and 1970s, when the state targeted dissidents with brutal tactics, including throwing victims from planes into the Pacific Ocean. In that era, forced disappearances—defined as the extrajudicial detention, killing, and concealment of bodies by state agents—were a tool of repression.

The problem evolved dramatically with the escalation of organized crime. Since the government declared war on drug cartels around 2006, disappearances have surged. The IACHR report notes that in the last 10 years alone, cases have increased by more than **200%**. Criminal gangs now use these tactics to terrorize communities, intimidate rivals, or dispose of evidence through methods like burning bodies, mass graves, or dissolving remains in acid.

While non-state actors, particularly organized crime, are responsible for the vast majority of cases, the report paints a grim picture of systemic involvement by authorities. It states that "many of the disappearances committed by organised crime occur in deep collusion and coordination with state agents." This includes direct actions, such as officials detaining individuals without warrants and handing them over to criminals, or indirect complicity, like ignoring crimes in progress.

The scale is staggering: more than **130,000** people reported missing, with the crisis concentrated in regions plagued by cartel violence. Relatives' efforts to search for loved ones have turned deadly, as evidenced by the May 10, 2026, march in Mexico City, where families demanded justice. Since 2010, at least **27** people actively looking for missing family members have been killed, underscoring the dangers faced by those challenging the status quo.

### State Collusion and Direct Involvement

The IACHR's sweeping investigation reveals that disappearances directly perpetrated by state agents "have not yet been eradicated." In some parts of the country, the number of such cases by government officials rivals those by criminals at certain times. The report highlights an "alarming" number of incidents involving torture, forced disappearances, and participation by state security actors.

Organized crime's infiltration of institutions is another key finding. Criminal groups recruit state agents responsible for security, law enforcement, and even political roles, creating a web of corruption that enables these crimes. This collusion allows gangs to operate with impunity, as officials either participate or turn a blind eye.

The report does not specify exact numbers for state-involved cases beyond describing them as "alarming," but it emphasizes the pattern's persistence despite international scrutiny. This builds on prior assessments, such as United Nations reports from the past year, which suggested enforced disappearances in Mexico may occur on a "widespread or systematic basis" and could constitute crimes against humanity.

### Government Response and Denials

Mexico's current leadership has firmly rejected these claims. President **Claudia Sheinbaum**, who took office in 2024, has repeatedly denied state responsibility. In response to a UN intimation last year about possible widespread enforced disappearances, she stated during a press conference: "In Mexico there is no forced disappearance by the state. We have fought against that all our lives; that does not exist in Mexico."

The government's reaction to a more recent UN statement last month was similarly defensive, dismissing the findings as "biased and dismissive." Activists argue this stance is part of a broader effort to downplay the crisis's severity. In March 2026, authorities released a report claiming that a third of disappearance cases lacked sufficient data, which critics see as an attempt to minimize the issue rather than address it.

While the IACHR report acknowledges that criminal gangs bear primary responsibility, it stresses that state complicity prevents eradication of the problem. No new policies or admissions from the government are mentioned in the investigation, leaving the response to these allegations unclear.

### What's Next

The IACHR report calls for urgent action, including stronger investigations into state involvement and protections for searchers. International pressure, via bodies like the UN, may intensify, but Mexico's rejection of external critiques suggests limited immediate change. Activists hope the exclusive access granted to media like **The Guardian** will amplify calls for accountability.

As the crisis persists, with no end to the drug war in sight, future reports may track progress—or lack thereof. Travelers and residents alike await meaningful reforms to address this "alarming" pattern, potentially through judicial overhauls or anti-corruption drives. For now, the more than **130,000** missing serve as a stark reminder of Mexico's unresolved human rights challenges.
