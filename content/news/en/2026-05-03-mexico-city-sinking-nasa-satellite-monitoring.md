Mexico City, home to more than **20 million people**, is sinking at alarming rates due to its location on a former lake bed above an ancient reservoir, a process documented for over a century. On May 1, 2026, [ABC News - Breaking News, Latest News and Videos](https://abcnews.com/International/mexico-city-sinking-century-new-nasa-satellite-now/story?id=132529109) reported that NASA's new U.S.-India NISAR satellite is now capturing this subsidence in near real-time, showing parts of the city dropping by more than half an inch every month. This enhanced monitoring, enabled by radar data collected every 12 days, aims to track the uneven ground movement that has long fractured roads, buildings, and water lines, offering scientists unprecedented tools to address the issue.

## Background on Mexico City's Subsidence

Mexico City's subsidence, or sinking of the land, stems from its unique geological foundation. The sprawling metropolis was built on the drained bed of Lake Texcoco, part of an ancient lacustrine plain that includes volcanic rock and soft sediments. Over the past century, excessive groundwater extraction to meet the needs of its massive population has accelerated the compaction of these underlying layers, causing the ground to sink unevenly. This has made Mexico City one of the fastest-sinking urban areas globally, with historical records confirming the phenomenon since at least the early 1900s.

Researchers like **Dora Carreón-Freyre**, who has studied the city's subsidence for more than 25 years, have witnessed the tangible effects firsthand, particularly in the Iztapalapa region, one of the most affected areas. "The houses that are founded in [volcanic] rock are stable, but the houses in the middle between the rock and the lacustrine plain are already broken, most of them," Carreón-Freyre told ABC News. She recounted a notable incident in 2017 when a taxi fell into a fracture caused by the sinking ground, highlighting the risks to daily life and infrastructure.

Prior to recent advancements, scientists relied on ground-based measurements and space satellites that provided only annual snapshots of the movement. These intermittent data points limited the ability to predict or mitigate sudden changes. The city's historic buildings, such as those in downtown areas, often appear tilted due to this uneven settling over decades or centuries, as seen in images from 2016 showing pedestrians passing by noticeably leaning structures.

The subsidence is not uniform; areas on firmer volcanic rock remain more stable, while softer lake bed zones subside faster, creating differential stresses that crack foundations and utilities.

## The Role of NASA's NISAR Satellite

The game-changer in monitoring this long-standing issue is the NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) satellite, a joint U.S.-India mission launched to observe Earth's changing surface. Unlike previous satellites, NISAR carries two radar systems operating at different wavelengths, allowing it to penetrate clouds and capture detailed ground deformation every 12 days. This frequency enables the creation of time-series data, showing how the terrain evolves over short periods.

NASA released satellite imagery on Wednesday, May 1, 2026, compiled from observations between October 25, 2025, and January 17, 2026. The data reveals subsidence rates of up to a few centimeters per month in blue-highlighted zones across Mexico City and its environs—equivalent to more than half an inch in some spots. **David Bekaert**, a scientist on the NISAR mission, emphasized the value of this granularity: "This all allows us to build time series or snapshots on how the ground is moving over time," he told ABC News.

For long-time researchers, this technology is transformative. Carreón-Freyre noted, "To have these tools and to realize the distribution of these differential rates—it's amazing. Things that we only learned by walking everywhere when we were young, it's different now. Technology is here to help us." The satellite's ability to provide near real-time insights marks a shift from reactive to proactive monitoring, potentially informing water management and construction practices.

This development aligns with broader global concerns about sinking cities, especially as a recent Nature study warns that 32 coastal cities, including some in the U.S., face heightened flooding risks from subsidence combined with rising sea levels. While Mexico City is inland, its subsidence exacerbates vulnerabilities to other environmental pressures, such as earthquakes on unstable ground. ## Impacts on Infrastructure and Daily Life

The cumulative effects of subsidence have been profound. Over decades, the incremental sinking—small on a monthly basis but significant over time—has led to widespread fracturing of roads, buildings, and water lines. In Iztapalapa and similar hard-hit areas, homes built on transitional zones between stable rock and soft plains show visible cracks, posing hazards to residents and visitors alike. The 2017 taxi incident underscores how these fissures can suddenly endanger lives and property.

Downtown landmarks, including historic buildings, tilt noticeably as the soft earth settles unevenly, a sight that has become a quirky feature for tourists but signals deeper structural concerns. Water infrastructure is particularly vulnerable; as the ground sinks, pipes strain and break, complicating the city's already challenged supply from the ancient reservoir below. This not only affects water access but also contributes to further subsidence by necessitating deeper pumping.

The NISAR data highlights the uneven nature of the problem, with some neighborhoods subsiding faster than others. This variability complicates urban development and maintenance, as engineers must account for ongoing movement in designs. For Mexico City, a hub of culture and history, these issues threaten preservation efforts. Visitors to sites like the Zócalo or Teotihuacan pyramids—though the latter is on firmer ground—should note that central areas may experience disruptions from repairs.
